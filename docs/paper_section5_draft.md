## 5.1 Data

**Legal corpus and KB.** The corpus contains 18 Vietnamese legal documents (laws, decrees and circulars on
social insurance, labour, enterprises and taxation; 106 chapters, 1,159 articles). An LLM extractor
(GPT-4o-mini, one call per article) produced 7,906 candidate rules. For each domain, the articles cited by the
benchmark questions were selected and their rules re-expressed over a shared predicate registry and reviewed,
giving five domain KBs with 636 rules in total (social insurance 149, enterprise 70, tax 200, labour 129,
cross-domain 88) and 422 encoded exception relations. Rules are Horn clauses over literals with an article-level
source; patches were derived **only from development-split failures**.

**Questions.** 300 Vietnamese application questions (social insurance 90, enterprise 70, tax 70, labour 30,
cross-domain 40), covering 225 distinct legal cases (the rest are paraphrases). Each question is annotated
with the legal goal, the case facts (with verbatim spans), the gold conclusion (yes 144 / no 85 /
insufficient 71) and its legal basis. "Insufficient" is defined by the law, independently of the KB:
(i) a missing general condition makes a case insufficient; (ii) an exception that the question does not raise
does not apply; (iii) an exception that is raised only partially makes the case insufficient. The questions and gold labels
were generated with GPT-4o from the cited provisions (they are synthetic, not collected user questions) and
checked against those provisions; an independent legal review of a stratified sample is ongoing.

**Splits.** Questions are split by legal case (paraphrases stay together) into development (194) and
held-out test (106) sets; all social-insurance questions were used to develop the KB and are development
only. All model choices (measurement model, prompt, acceptance threshold) were made on the development set.

**Diagnostic instances.** (a) *Controlled fault injection*: from every question whose gold conclusion the KB
reproduces, single-fault instances are generated for each layer — a fact removed from the question
(Missing Case Information), removed from the parse (Parser Omission), negated (Parser Misinterpretation),
an unstated fact added (Unsupported Parser Fact), and, where two encoded rules compete, the overlap kept or
its encoded exception removed (Resolvable Rule Overlap / Unresolved KB Conflict); an instance is kept only
if the fault is observable in the reasoning (1,499 development / 451 test instances). (b) *External fault
set*: 180 F1–F4 fixtures built independently of the KB (faults chosen from the question annotation only)
plus 20 fault-free controls; 166 are usable (14 excluded because the KB does not reproduce the source
question or the source label was revised), 134 of them with an observable fault.

## 5.2 Compared systems and setup

- **GPT-4o (pure LLM)**: question only, fixed prompt, temperature 0; returns a verdict and an IRAC answer.
  No KB, no retrieval, no parsed or gold facts.
- **Symbolic QA**: GPT-4o semantic parser → domain KB → three-valued forward reasoner; no diagnosis.
- **Heuristic diagnosis**: Symbolic QA plus an IF–THEN classifier over *the same evidence* (accepted NLI
  measurements of all relevant propositions, KB verification of all competing pairs) and the same recovery.
- **Ours (cross-layer MBD)**: Symbolic QA plus consistency-based diagnosis in SWI-Prolog over user,
  parser and KB components, diagnosis-directed measurement (first-disagreement policy), clarification on
  missing-vs-omitted ambiguity, and layer-ordered recovery.

All systems use the gold legal goal; end-to-end results are therefore end-to-end *under an oracle goal*. **Measurement model.** The NLI measurement of a
proposition is the mean label distribution of mDeBERTa-v3 (XNLI) and GPT-4o-mini (logprobs of the three
option letters, one prompt, temperature 0); a label becomes an observation only if its probability is at
least τ = 0.50, otherwise the measurement yields ⊥ and adds no observation. Model, prompt and τ were chosen
on the development split only (criterion fixed in advance: mean of diagnosis accuracy and 1 − false-alarm
rate). Diagnosis search enumerates set-minimal diagnoses by increasing cardinality up to 5 faults, lowering
the bound when the candidate space exceeds 2·10⁵. In the fault-injection benchmark a simulated user
answers clarification requests with the gold case facts; in end-to-end QA no user is simulated and a
clarification request counts as the answer "insufficient".

## 5.3 Metrics

QA accuracy (verdict = gold); diagnosis accuracy (the first diagnosis is exactly the injected fault, or no
fault on controls) and per-category precision/recall/F1; **correct action** (diagnosis correct, or the only
open choice is missing-vs-omitted on the true proposition and the system asks the user about exactly it);
recovery gain (QA accuracy on faulty inputs minus that of Symbolic QA); false-alarm rate on fault-free
controls; and cost: NLI measurements and **clarification requests per query**. 95% confidence intervals are
percentile bootstrap intervals (1,000 resamples; on the external set, source questions are resampled).
**IRAC quality** is scored on 40 stratified test questions with the rubric
IRAC = 100·(0.10·I + 0.35·R + 0.40·A + 0.15·C), each component 0 / 0.5 / 1; language is not scored.
Answers are anonymised and shuffled; the judge is GPT-4.1 (not the GPT-4o baseline) and sees the gold
conclusion and legal basis; 20 questions are also scored by a human to report agreement.

Judged by the standard criteria of model-based diagnosis — every reported fault must be a real one — MBD is
significantly more precise than the matched heuristic (fault F1 +10.6 points on single faults), with the same

non-faulty conditions 2.0–3.4 times less per query. The heuristic's higher instance-level diagnosis
accuracy (Table 2) comes from listing many candidate faults (3.07 per diagnosis, up to 36), which that
lenient metric does not penalise.

diagnosis accuracy [51, 87] and 73.0% correct action with 0.55 clarifications per query, against 73.0%

**Discussion.**
(1) *Diagnosis-guided recovery is what makes the symbolic pipeline usable*: with a single fault per input,
Symbolic QA answers 4.3% of faulty inputs correctly; diagnosis and recovery raise this to 85.7% (+81.5 pp).
Replacing consistency-based diagnosis by a direct symptom-to-cause mapping loses 28.1 pp of QA accuracy and
26.4 pp of diagnosis accuracy.
(2) *MBD versus a matched IF–THEN classifier.* With identical evidence and single faults, MBD and the
heuristic reach the same QA accuracy (85.1%) and comparable diagnosis quality (macro-F1 66.8 vs 67.4;
correct action 68.3 vs 68.5%), with the same measurement effort (5.6 vs 5.8 NLI measurements and 0.37 KB
checks per query); under an equal clarification budget the two stay within about one point (Table 6). The
difference lies in interaction: the heuristic turns every unresolved condition into a "missing information"
diagnosis and asks about it (3.71 clarification requests per query, 11.8 on the tax test set), whereas MBD
asks only when the remaining set-minimal diagnoses are genuinely indistinguishable (1.70 per query, 4.8 on
tax). The diagnosis-accuracy metric tolerates extra faults that are true of the instance (an unstated
condition reported as missing); the heuristic benefits more from this (34 of its 309 correct diagnoses
contain extra faults, 25 of 298 for MBD). Requiring the diagnosis to be exactly the injected fault (a
secondary, stricter analysis) gives 61.4% for the heuristic and 61.0% for MBD, and the heuristic's
diagnoses are a third larger (3.12 vs 2.11 faults on average, up to 36): set-minimality keeps MBD's
explanations parsimonious. On single-fault inputs we therefore claim parity in accuracy at roughly half the user interaction,
together with explicit ambiguity and set-minimal, observation-consistent explanations, not higher accuracy.
Two design points were necessary for this (identified on the development set before testing): an encoded,
resolvable overlap must not end the search for an input fault, and when the bounded joint search finds no
diagnosis, independent components (one proposition or rule pair each) are diagnosed separately and combined,
which is exact because every model constraint involves a single component.
(For transparency: an earlier controller without these two points had been run on the test set
(QA 81.6%, macro-F1 62.1%, 0.35 clarifications/query); the fixes were derived from development-set errors
only, and the test set was re-run once.)
*Two simultaneous faults* (Table 7; cross-layer pairs fixed before running: missing + misinterpreted,
omitted + misinterpreted, missing + unsupported, misinterpreted + unsupported). Both diagnosers localise
both faults in 59.6% of test cases and reach the same QA accuracy (81.7%), again with less than half of the
heuristic's clarification requests (2.38 vs 5.79 per query). Both find the second fault in a later recovery
round rather than in the first diagnosis, because input faults on the proof are verified before unresolved
conditions are explained; the first-match heuristic loses no accuracy here because it acts on all of its
labels at once and the simulated user answers every question. A direct symptom-to-cause mapping fails
almost completely (1.9%).

(3) *The bottleneck is measurement, not the diagnostic logic.* With perfect measurements, MBD diagnoses 100%
of the observable external faults and 88–100% overall; with real NLI the gap is entirely explained by
measurement errors (in an error analysis on the development set with the mDeBERTa + Qwen measurement,
20 of 24 remaining errors trace back to a wrong or rejected NLI label). Diagnosis quality follows measurement quality (Table 4): the best measurement roughly doubles
diagnosis accuracy over the XNLI classifier alone. QA accuracy alone is a misleading criterion here: with the
over-cautious open-weight Qwen2.5-7B ("not mentioned" for stated facts), the system wrongly suspects
missing information and asks the user 4.4 times per query, which the omniscient simulated user rewards with
80% QA accuracy despite 32% correct diagnoses. Averaging a classifier that is precise on stated facts with
an LLM that is precise on unstated ones gives the best diagnoses with the fewest clarifications.
(4) *GPT-4o versus proof-grounded answers (Table 8).* End to end, with the real GPT-4o parser (fact
precision 88.9%, recall 84.7%) and no simulated user, the pure GPT-4o baseline is more accurate on the
held-out test questions (70.8% vs 65.1% for Ours and 66.0% for the heuristic). The symbolic systems are
far better on the development questions (85.6% vs 71.7%), on which the KBs were completed, so the test gap
reflects KB coverage of unseen cases (the KBs reproduce the gold conclusion for 92/106 test questions under
gold facts) and parser recall, not the diagnosis. The systems fail differently (all 300 questions): GPT-4o answers "insufficient"
correctly in only 52.1% of such questions (it tends to commit to yes/no), while the diagnosing systems reach
70.4%; on yes-questions GPT-4o is weaker than MBD (82.6 vs 88.2%). IRAC quality is similar (71.2 for
GPT-4o, 70.0 for Ours, 71.1 heuristic, 68.8 symbolic): GPT-4o states the issue better (I = 1.0 vs 0.71,
the KB-grounded answers phrase the issue from a predicate template) and argues more fluently (A 0.79 vs
0.73), whereas the proof-grounded answers cite the governing provisions more accurately (R 0.64 vs 0.55;
GPT-4o often cites superseded laws, several 2024–2025 statutes postdating its training data) and reach the
correct conclusion more often (C 0.78 vs 0.70). The narrow IRAC gap comes from the R component. GPT-4o names a specific article in
only 22.5% of its answers ("under the Law on Social Insurance, …") and cites 12.5% of the gold articles,
against 77.5% and 62.5% for the proof-grounded answers, yet the judge gave such unspecific citations 0.5:
the pre-registered rubric (v1) does not separate "correct law, a key provision missing" (0.5) from "key
provisions missing" (0). We therefore made R precise after the first scoring (rubric v2: R = 1 requires the
correct document and article numbers of the governing provisions; 0.5 at least one correct article;
0 no article number, a wrong article or a superseded law), left I, A and C unchanged, and re-scored all
four methods with the same judge. Under v2 the IRAC scores are 57.7 (GPT-4o), 60.8 (Symbolic QA),
64.6 (Heuristic) and 63.7 (Ours), with R = 0.18 for GPT-4o against 0.46–0.51 for the KB-grounded systems.
In the objective variant, which removes the judge from the two components that can be checked
mechanically, the proof-grounded answers score highest (Ours 69.1, Heuristic 68.0, Symbolic 64.5) and
GPT-4o lowest (55.6): its answers state the issue and argue fluently (I = 1.00, A = 0.79) but rarely cite
the governing articles (R = 0.13). All three variants are reported; the human scoring of 20 questions
(pending) is used to check the judged components.

On the full set the picture of the 40-question sample holds: the proof-grounded answers score 8–18
points above pure GPT-4o under both the v2 and the objective scoring, driven by the Rule component
(0.68 vs 0.11 of answers citing the governing articles), while GPT-4o leads on Issue and is close on
Application; MBD and the heuristic produce answers of equal IRAC quality (within 1.5 points), as expected
since both verbalise the same verified proof and differ only in the diagnosis that precedes it.

**Limitations.** Gold labels are LLM-generated and still under legal review; faults are single and
injected; the simulated user knows all case facts; the legal goal is given (oracle goal); the test split
is small (106 questions), so per-domain differences are not significant.
